# Signs of AI writing

Wikipedia editors maintain a field guide to the patterns that mark text as
machine-generated:
[Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing).
This file summarizes the patterns that matter outside Wikipedia. Read the
full guide for examples and the research behind each pattern.

The guide treats these patterns as signs, not proof. Use them to find weak
prose in your own drafts, not to accuse anyone of using AI.

## Why the patterns appear

Language models predict likely text, so they drift toward what is
statistically common. Specific, unusual facts give way to generic, positive
description. The result reads fluently and says little.

## Content

- Inflated significance: claims that a subject "stands as a testament,"
  "plays a pivotal role," or "reflects broader trends." State what the thing
  does instead.
- Tacked-on analysis: a sentence that ends in an "-ing" phrase, such as
  "ensuring reliability" or "highlighting its importance." Cut the phrase, or
  turn it into a claim you can support.
- Promotional tone: "groundbreaking," "seamless," "boasts." Describe; don't
  sell.
- Lecturing disclaimers: "It's important to note that…" Say the thing.
- Reflexive summaries: a closing paragraph that opens with "In summary" or
  "Overall" and repeats what came before.
- Formulaic challenges: "Despite its strengths, X faces challenges…"
  followed by vague optimism.
- Vague attribution: "experts say" or "industry reports suggest," with no
  named source.

## Language

- Overused vocabulary: delve, crucial, enhance, foster, intricate,
  landscape, pivotal, showcase, tapestry, underscore, and their relatives.
  One is harmless; several together form a pattern.
- Negative parallelism: "It's not just X, it's Y" and "Not only… but also…"
- Lists of negatives: "No setup, no config, just results."
- Reflexive triplets: adjectives or phrases grouped in threes to make thin
  analysis look thorough.
- Elegant variation: a new synonym for the same thing in every sentence. In
  technical writing, use one term for one thing.
- False ranges: "from X to Y" where X and Y aren't the ends of any scale.
- Title case in headings where the style calls for sentence case.

## Formatting

- Bold scattered across phrases for emphasis.
- Lists whose items each open with a bold label and a colon, used where
  prose would read better.
- Emoji in headings or list items.
- Em dashes where a comma, colon, or parentheses would do. Chainguard style
  uses spaced em dashes, so the sign is overuse, not the mark itself.
- Curly and straight quotation marks mixed in one text.

## Leftover chat

Text meant for the person who wrote the prompt, not for the reader: "I hope
this helps," "Certainly!", "Let me know if you'd like…", knowledge-cutoff
hedges such as "as of my last update," refusals, and unfilled placeholders
such as "[Your product name]".

## Fabrication

Citations, links, and identifiers that don't resolve: invented references,
dead links, and invalid DOIs or ISBNs. Check every source and link in an
AI-assisted draft.

## What isn't a sign

Correct grammar is not a sign, and neither is any single pattern on its own.
The guide stresses that combinations matter, and that skilled human writers
produce some of these patterns too.
